## Б. Сценарий

### 01 · You, in ordinary life · 0:00–0:35 · 87 слов

> There's an email in your inbox. It would take maybe twenty minutes to answer. It's been sitting there for nine days.
> In those nine days, you've cleaned out the fridge. You've reorganized your desktop. You've watched an entire documentary about octopuses.
> Every morning you tell yourself: today. And every morning, the moment you see that subject line, something small tightens in your chest. And you click away.
> And here's the strange part. It's not some boring email you don't care about. It's the one that matters most.

*Визуал:* герой ночью у ноутбука, непрочитанное письмо; быстрые склейки: холодильник, рабочий стол, осьминог на экране; утро — герой отводит взгляд от экрана.

### 02 · The main question · 0:35–1:00 · 64 слов

> So what's going on?
> If you didn't care, avoiding it would make sense. But you do care. A lot. And somehow, that's exactly the one you can't open.
> The usual verdict is simple. You're lazy. You have no discipline. You just need to try harder.
> But lazy people don't lie awake feeling guilty about an email. Lazy people don't care. And you clearly do.

*Визуал:* карточка LAZY? (без красного креста — это сомнение, а не опровержение); герой ворочается в кровати, над головой висит письмо.

### 03 · The promise · 1:00–1:16 · 40 слов

> A growing body of research points somewhere else. Procrastination often isn't a time problem. It's a feeling problem.
> Let's look at three things your brain is doing when you put off what matters, and what the evidence says actually helps.

*Визуал:* часы, которые превращаются в сердце/эмоцию (метафора); карточка 3 THINGS.

### 04 · What procrastination is, and the first experiment · 1:16–3:12 · 288 слов

> First, let's be precise. Not every delay is procrastination. Waiting until you have more information is just smart.
> The psychologist Piers Steel defines procrastination as voluntarily delaying something you intended to do, even though you expect to be worse off for the delay.
> That last part is the key. You know it will cost you. You do it anyway.
> In 2007, Steel pulled together close to seven hundred findings on procrastination. One of the strongest patterns wasn't about character. It was about the task: the more unpleasant it felt, the more likely people were to put it off.
> Researchers Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl describe procrastination as a way of repairing your mood right now, and handing the bill to your future self.
> One clever experiment shows just how central that is.
> In 2001, Dianne Tice, Ellen Bratslavsky, and Roy Baumeister had students read a short, upsetting story and imagine being in it. Then they were told a math test was coming and given fifteen minutes to practice. Nearby sat a puzzle, a video game, and some magazines.
> But first, the researcher lit an aromatherapy candle. Some students were told it would freeze their mood. As long as it burned, nothing they did would make them feel better.
> Students in a bad mood, with fun distractions in the room, spent almost fourteen of those fifteen minutes not practicing. But when they believed their mood was frozen, that dropped to under six minutes.
> When feeling better was off the table, the reason to procrastinate disappeared.
> It was a small lab study, so it can't explain every delay. But it points to something important: a lot of procrastination isn't about the task. It's about the feeling the task gives you.

*Визуал:* СХЕМА «delay ≠ procrastination»; карточка цитаты-определения (в монтаже); СХЕМА эксперимента: история → свеча → 15 минут → столбики 13.7 vs 5.7 мин.

### 05 · You, inside the mechanism · 3:12–4:10 · 145 слов

> Let's slow it down and watch it happen to you.
> You open your inbox. You see the subject line. That's the event.
> In a fraction of a second, your mind adds a story. What if my reply sounds stupid? What if they're already annoyed that I'm late? That's the interpretation.
> Then comes the feeling. Not panic. Just a quick, sharp flicker of dread.
> So you react. A new tab. A quick look at your phone. Just one video.
> And here's the trap. It works. The dread fades almost instantly. And your brain learns a simple lesson: avoiding that email makes me feel better.
> But the email doesn't fade. Every day it waits, it gets heavier. Now you're not just replying. You're replying late. Now you need an apology.
> The task grows, the dread grows, and the escape gets more tempting.
> Relief now. Interest added later.

*Визуал:* СХЕМА-петля из 5 шагов (event → story → feeling → escape → relief), письмо, которое каждый день тяжелеет (метафора: камень в рюкзаке).

### 06 · "But I work better under pressure" · 4:10–5:27 · 194 слов

> But maybe you're thinking: that's not me. I actually work better under pressure.
> In 1997, Dianne Tice and Roy Baumeister followed college students through a semester-long health psychology course.
> Early on, the procrastinators looked like the winners. They reported less stress and fewer symptoms of illness than the other students.
> Then the deadlines arrived. By the end of the semester, the procrastinators reported more stress, more symptoms, and more visits to health professionals. Their grades were lower too.
> The researchers called it a self-defeating pattern, with short-term benefits and long-term costs.
> That was a small study. But in 2023, a much larger one followed more than three and a half thousand university students in Sweden for nine months.
> Students who procrastinated more at the start were slightly more likely, nine months later, to report symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress. Also poor sleep, loneliness, money trouble, and disabling pain in the shoulders and arms.
> Slightly is the honest word. The researchers called these links weak, and a study like this can't prove that procrastination caused any of it. But it lines up with the 1997 study. The relief is real. So is the bill.

*Визуал:* СРАВНЕНИЕ «начало семестра / конец семестра»; временная шкала 9 месяцев; карточка 3,525 STUDENTS.

### 07 · Three things your brain is doing · 5:27–8:04 · 391 слов

> So why does this hit hardest with the things you care about most? Here are three things your brain is doing.
> One. It's reacting to the feeling, not the task.
> In a study by Allan Blunt and Timothy Pychyl, students rated projects they were working on and projects they were putting off. The ones they avoided tended to feel boring, frustrating, or resented. Often they felt meaningless. And when it was time to actually do them, the steps felt unclear.
> Notice what's not on that list. "I'm a bad person." Your brain isn't judging your character. It's reacting to a signal: this is going to feel bad. And it steers away.
> Two. The more it matters, the more it threatens.
> This answers our question.
> In a 2000 study, Joseph Ferrari and Dianne Tice gave students a math task, plus time beforehand to practice, or to play with a puzzle or a video game instead. Some were told the task was a meaningful test of their cognitive skills. Others were told the same task was just a fun game.
> Chronic procrastinators practiced less, but only when the task was framed as a test of their ability. When it was just a game, they practiced like everyone else.
> Think about what that buys you. If you start late and do badly, you can always say: well, I barely tried. Psychologists call this self-handicapping. It protects your self-image, at the cost of your results.
> And it's not ambition itself. A 2017 analysis of forty-four studies found that simply setting high standards went with slightly less procrastination. The link came from the anxious side of perfectionism: worrying about mistakes, and about what others think.
> So when you care, the task can stop being just a task. It becomes a verdict on you. And verdicts are scary.
> Three. Your brain treats future you a bit like someone else.
> Emily Pronin and her colleagues asked students to drink a disgusting mix of ketchup, soy sauce, and water, for science. Deciding for right now, they chose about two tablespoons. Deciding for their future selves, next semester, they signed up for about half a cup. The same amount they chose for another student.
> So when you say "I'll do it tomorrow," you're not just delaying. You're handing the dread to someone your brain treats a little less like you.

*Визуал:* три карточки ONE / TWO / THREE; список свойств «отталкивающей» задачи иконками; СРАВНЕНИЕ «test of ability / fun game»; щит перед героем (самозащита); СХЕМА перфекционизма: «high standards» vs «fear of mistakes»; стакан бурой жидкости — 2 ложки / полстакана; герой передаёт груз своему «будущему» силуэту.

### 08 · A question for you · 8:04–8:30 · 65 слов

> Try this. Think about the last thing you put off. Not the task itself. The moment right before you avoided it.
> What was the feeling? Boredom? Not knowing where to start? Or the fear that whatever you made wouldn't be good enough?
> You don't have to agree with anything I've said. Just check. Were you avoiding the work, or the feeling that came with it?

*Визуал:* герой в паузе перед дверью/экраном; три иконки чувств.

### 09 · The twist · 8:30–9:44 · 187 слов

> Now here's where a lot of advice gets it backwards.
> If procrastination is a lack of discipline, the obvious fix is to be harder on yourself. More pressure. More guilt. More "what is wrong with you?"
> But look at what guilt does inside the loop. It's one more bad feeling attached to the task. And the brain's favorite way to escape a bad feeling is to avoid the task.
> In 2010, Michael Wohl, Timothy Pychyl, and Shannon Bennett followed a hundred and nineteen first-year students across two midterms. Before the first, they measured how hard students were on themselves for procrastinating.
> Among students who had procrastinated a lot, the ones who forgave themselves procrastinated less before the second exam. Part of the reason seemed to be that they felt less bad about the first one.
> It's one study, and it can't prove cause and effect. But it fits a wider pattern. Fuschia Sirois found that people who procrastinate more tend to be less compassionate toward themselves, and more stressed.
> The harsh voice that's supposed to keep you in line may be part of what keeps you stuck.

*Визуал:* внутренний критик — тёмная фигура-мегафон за плечом героя; петля из блока 05, в которую добавляется «вина» и петля раскручивается сильнее; два экзамена на временной шкале.

### 10 · What you can change · 9:44–11:06 · 205 слов

> So what can you actually do? Three things worth trying. None of them is magic.
> First, shrink the start until it stops setting off the alarm. Not "answer the email." Just "open it and write one clumsy sentence." The goal isn't to finish. It's to get past the moment of dread, because that's where procrastination wins. This one follows from the research more than it's been tested on its own.
> Second, make an if-then plan. "If it's nine a.m. and I've poured my coffee, then I open the email." A 2024 review of more than six hundred tests found that plans like this do help people follow through. The effect is smaller than earlier studies suggested, but it's real.
> Third, when you slip, forgive the slip. Not "it doesn't matter." More like: "I avoided it because it felt bad. That's human. What's the next small step?"
> And if putting things off is seriously hurting your work, your health, or your relationships, it can be tangled up with things like anxiety, depression, or attention difficulties. A video can't diagnose that. A professional can help.
> In one randomized trial in Sweden, a ten-week online program based on cognitive behavioral therapy reduced procrastination compared with a waiting list.

*Визуал:* огромная задача → крошечная первая ступенька; карточка IF → THEN; рука на плече героя; схема «10 weeks».

### 11 · The short version · 11:06–11:29 · 57 слов

> So here's the short version.
> You're not lazy. You're trying to feel better, right now.
> Procrastination works for five minutes, and charges you for weeks.
> The more you care, the more the task feels like a verdict.
> And the way out is less about forcing yourself, and more about making the start feel safe enough to begin.

*Визуал:* четыре короткие карточки подряд, по одной на фразу.

### 12 · Back to the email · 11:29–12:22 · 131 слов

> Let's go back to that email. Day ten.
> You open your inbox. You see the subject line. You feel the flicker.
> But this time, you notice it. Oh. There it is. That's the feeling.
> You don't fight it. You don't scold yourself. You just write one sentence. "Hi, sorry for the slow reply."
> And maybe the thing you've been running from for ten days turns out to be smaller than it was in your head.
> You were never really avoiding the email. You were avoiding a feeling. And feelings, unlike emails, pass on their own.
>
> What's the thing you've been putting off? You don't have to say what it is. Just tell me the feeling behind it: boredom, confusion, or the fear of not being good enough. I read the comments.

*Визуал:* повтор кадра из начала, но светлее; герой печатает; камень из рюкзака уменьшается; финальный кадр — отправленное письмо. Вопрос в комментарии — после паузы, на отдельном кадре.

## Д. Проверка сценария

- **Выдуманные факты:** нет. Все числа взяты из первоисточников; спорные детали исключены. Одна формулировка помечена как недоказанная прямо в тексте: совет «уменьши первый шаг» назван выводом из исследований, а не проверенным методом.
- **Корреляция и причина:** исследования 1997, 2010, 2014, 2017, 2023 годов — корреляционные; в тексте везде «linked», «tend to», «can't prove». Причинные выводы сделаны только по экспериментам (свеча, «тест vs игра», напиток).
- **Преувеличение выводов:** эксперимент со свечой назван «small lab study»; эффект если-то планов — «smaller than earlier studies suggested»; КПТ — «one randomized trial».
